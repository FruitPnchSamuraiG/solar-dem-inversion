## 1. Introduction

The differential emission measure (DEM) says how much plasma lies at each temperature along a line of sight. It is a key diagnostic of coronal thermal structure, separating the warm corona near 1 MK (logT 6.0) from flare plasma near 8 MK (logT 6.9). The Atmospheric Imaging Assembly (AIA) on the Solar Dynamics Observatory (SDO) images the full disk at 4096 × 4096 pixels in six EUV channels (94, 131, 171, 193, 211 and 335 Å), each sensitive to a different mix of temperatures, so a DEM can be estimated for every pixel of every image.

Six intensities constrain a DEM on 18 temperature bins, so many DEMs fit the observations within their noise, and the solver's tie-break decides the shape it reports (section 2). Two solvers are the references here. Basis Pursuit (BP) returns the DEM with the smallest total basis weight that reproduces every channel within a noise band, one linear program per pixel. ElasticNet fits the same basis by least squares with L1 and L2 penalties on the weights. BP follows Cheung et al. (2015) and the ElasticNet form Athiray and Winebarger (2024); DeepEM (Wright et al. 2019) first trained a network on BP's DEMs.

Both solve every pixel separately, which is what makes them expensive. One image on our 2048 × 2048 grid (every second AIA pixel in each direction) is 4.2 million problems. BP solved about 1,400 pixels per second per CPU core; with the deconvolution and the retries at relaxed tolerances, a BP job took about 9.5 minutes per image on 16 cores and an ElasticNet job about 5.25. Clean BP and ElasticNet DEMs for our 1,223 images took 2,446 such jobs and 1.74 TB of output.

Supervised emulators move this cost into training. DeepEM trained a network on solver DEMs, and the supervised side's emulator modernises it with Fourier-feature inputs, a wider SiLU network and a binned distribution over each output. Inference is one forward pass per pixel, but the model stays bound to its labels. It needs solver DEMs for every training image. A change of solver settings invalidates them: 886 GB of our first ElasticNet DEMs (α = 1) were superseded when the supervised side's α = 0.001 was confirmed. And it learns the solver's answer even where that answer is questionable: at the 4.4% of pixels whose 171 or 193 Å value the deconvolution clamped to zero, BP still fits a DEM to the artefact (section 7.2).

We instead train on the solver's objective, which depends only on the observations and their noise. This is amortised optimisation: one network learns the map from an observation to its solution instead of solving each pixel afresh, and it never sees a solver DEM. The objective must be the solver's own: in an early per-pixel test, losses that only fit the six channels reproduced AIA about 12 times better than BP yet landed about 5 times further from its DEMs (appendix A). The network reads one pixel's six intensities and outputs the solvers' 54 basis weights, from which the fixed basis and response matrix give the DEM and the predicted intensities. For ElasticNet the loss is the solver's objective with its settings; for BP it is the sparsity term with the band as a penalty, zero inside and quadratic outside, weighted by the band weight μ (section 3). When the ElasticNet setting changed, the model was simply retrained. Solver DEMs enter only the evaluation, on the supervised side's protocol: both models are scored on the same pixels of 153 test images (September to December 2015) against the same solver DEMs (section 4); ours trains on 917 earlier images. The work is AIA-only; XRT and the uncertainty output are left open, and the network's speed against the solver is unmeasured (section 9).

This report makes four claims.

1. **Label-free training matches or beats a supervised emulator.** A 409k-parameter MLP on one pixel's six intensities, trained only on the solver's objective, is ahead of the supervised model on every ElasticNet measure and on four of six BP measures, and three times closer to BP on the typical pixel (median pixel error 0.036 against 0.112; section 5).
2. **The one BP failure was in the loss.** At flare cores the network sat close to its loss's per-pixel optimum (a median 8% above it), and at μ = 1 that optimum leaves out the hot plasma. A stiffer band (μ = 100) with longer training puts it back: flare-core emission rises from 40% to 93% of the solver's (section 6). μ need not be tuned blind: penalty-method theory places each channel λ_c tσ_c / (2μ) half-widths outside its band at the optimum, with λ_c the channel's dual price in BP's linear program, so μ follows from the response matrix, the noise and a chosen tolerance (section 3.3).
3. **Bigger is not better.** At scale the per-pixel MLP beats the patch CNN, and larger MLPs improve the typical pixel a little while, on the stiff band and on ElasticNet, blowing up on a few rare pixels (section 5.4); section 7.1 shows they are inputs that training excluded, lone single-channel spikes among channels zeroed by the deconvolution, where a larger network extrapolates further.
4. **The remaining error is concentrated.** Flare cores, where some channel reaches 32 times its Bright cutoff, are one pixel in 10,000 yet hold 94% of the label-free model's BP squared error and 90% of the supervised model's (section 5.2). The outliers at the low end of the AIA joint distributions are about 50,000 pixels, 0.007% of the test set: deconvolution-clamp zeros, where the solver's DEM disagrees with the observation as much as ours, and the lone spikes above (section 7.2).
