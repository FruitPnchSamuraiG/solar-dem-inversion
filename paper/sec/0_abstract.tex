\begin{abstract}
The differential emission measure (DEM) records how much plasma lies at each temperature along a line of sight through the solar corona. Classical solvers recover it from extreme-ultraviolet images by solving one constrained optimization per pixel, about four million problems per image. Supervised emulators make this fast but need solver output for every training image. We instead train a per-pixel network on the solver's own objective, so no solver DEM is ever a training target. For Basis Pursuit this turns a hard noise band into a squared penalty, and the penalty weight then shapes the answer. Above a threshold computable from the linear program, each channel settles outside its band by an amount set by its dual price and noise, falling as $1/\mu$. Below it the solution changes support, and at lower weights hot flare plasma is lost. At 10,899 held-out pixels the loss's numerical minimizer matches this prediction (median ratio 1.00), and the analysis shows why more training could not fix the failure we first saw at flare cores. On 153 held-out images our models compare well with a supervised emulator. On Elastic Net they lead on five of six headline measures and tie the sixth. On Basis Pursuit they lead on four of six, with a median pixel error 3.6 times lower, while the supervised model keeps lower DEM MSE (0.91 against 1.08) and AIA MAE (2.95 against 3.54).
\end{abstract}
